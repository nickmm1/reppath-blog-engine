I have been on both sides of the medical sales interview table for over 20 years. I have interviewed for roles, I have hired reps as a sales manager, and now I run job postings where a single opening pulls in 300 to 500 applications. So when I tell you how competitive this is, I am not guessing. I am looking at the stack of resumes on my desk every single week.

Here is the one idea that changes everything about how you prepare. The entire interview process is a sales call. From the second you send your resume to the second you close your follow up, you are the product and you are always being evaluated. Most candidates forget that. The ones who remember it get hired.

This is the full blueprint I walk my RepPath clients through. It is the same process that gets people offers over candidates who look better on paper. None of it is complicated. All of it takes work.

Treat the Whole Process Like a Sales Call

Every company is watching how you handle the process because that is exactly how they expect you to handle a real sales call once you have the job. If you are slow, sloppy, and passive with them, they assume you will be slow, sloppy, and passive with their customers. If you are sharp, persistent, and prepared, they assume that is how you will show up in the field.

So you sell yourself the entire way through. You do it when you reach out to reps who are not the hiring manager, because those reps talk to the hiring manager. You do it in every email, every call, every follow up. You never stop, because the evaluation never stops. Keep that in your head from the first application to the final offer.

Apply to Fewer Jobs, Not More

This is where I lose people, so stay with me. When I started almost 25 years ago, the game was spray and pray. You blasted your resume to every opening you could find and prayed somebody called back. Years ago that actually worked. Today it is dead.

My advice is the opposite of what most people do. Apply to fewer jobs. If you used to apply to 100, apply to 10 a week, and go all in on three to five of them. Each application becomes a full court press where you learn everything you can about the manager, the company, the culture, and the territory. If you are not willing to go 100 percent on a specific opening, do not send your resume to it. You are just wasting your time and setting yourself up to get ghosted.

One filter before you apply: make sure you meet at least 75 percent of the listed requirements. Years ago I would have said 60 percent was fine. It is too competitive for that now. If the posting asks for a four year degree and you do not have one, do not apply. When you keep applying to jobs you are not qualified for, nobody responds, you get frustrated, and you lose your motivation. I have been there. Protect your energy for the openings you can actually win.

Make the Recruiter's Job Easy

When you do apply, do not just send a resume with a note that says you are interested. When I am working 30 or 40 openings at once and a resume lands with the message "Joe, I'm interested," I do not even know which job you mean. That is the fastest way to get ignored.

Here is what to do instead. In the body of your message, tell me the exact role you are applying for, then paste the requirements and put a short bullet under each one in a different color showing how you meet it. Keep the bullets tight:

  • Degree requirement: Bachelor of Science in Biology, Montclair State University, 2010.
  • B2B sales requirement: Two years of B2B sales at Cintas.
  • Territory or product requirement: One line that proves the match.

Good rule of thumb. If I open your message on my phone and have to scroll with my finger, it is too long. A recruiter's job is to make life easy for the hiring manager. Your job as a candidate is to make life easy for the recruiter. Do that and you stand out before anyone has even met you.

Get Three Conversations Before the First Interview

This is the single highest leverage move in the whole process, and almost nobody does it. Before I ever sat in front of a hiring manager, my goal was to have at least three real conversations with people at that company first.

Say HR tells you there is an opening in the endoscopy division at a device company in New York City. Go to LinkedIn, search the company, division, and city, and you will see the directors, managers, and reps come up. Reach out to as many of them as it takes. Use a short, personalized message: "Hey Susan, I just applied for the New York City endoscopy role. I'm excited about it and would love to pick your brain for a couple of minutes. Does Thursday or Friday work better for you?" Give them two options instead of asking what works. That is old school sales for a reason.

Three conversations does not mean message three people. It might mean messaging 50 to land three. That is fine. You are copying and pasting the same blurb and swapping the name at the top. The goals are simple. Learn what the hiring manager cares about, learn the real challenges of the territory, and get on the radar. Because those reps go back to the manager and say, "This candidate reached out to me." When the manager hears that from three or four people before you even walk in, you are the person they have to meet.

I watched this happen with a rep going for a pain position against two candidates who were flat out studs. On paper he had no shot. He outworked both of them during the process. He had so many conversations, learned the territory cold, and walked in ready to go on day one. He got the offer, because he showed them he would put in that same effort once he had the job.

Send a Thank You Before the Interview

Everyone sends a thank you after the interview. Almost nobody sends one before. Try it.

If you know you are meeting Bob Smith next week, get his email. If you have been doing your homework and talking to reps, you can usually piece the company email format together. Then send something short: "Hey Bob, I know we're meeting tomorrow at 4:30. I'm excited about the opportunity. Is there anything you'd like me to prepare ahead of time?" That is it. Combine that with the reach outs to the team and you are already showing your work ethic and your excitement before you have said a word in person. I still do not know many reps who send a thank you beforehand, and I know nobody who does both. Do both, every time.

Show Me, Don't Tell Me

Never walk into an interview and say "nobody outworks me" or "I'm a hard worker." Those words are worthless on their own. The first thing I ask is, "What did you do to prepare for this interview?" If your answer is that you researched the company, built a territory plan, reached out to the team, and had real conversations, you just proved it. You showed me instead of telling me.

That is also why I am not a fan of a generic 30 60 90 day plan. If you hand me a plan you whipped up in a chatbot with no knowledge of the territory, it means nothing. But if you use those three conversations to learn the accounts, the competition, and the low hanging fruit, and then build a plan that names real hospitals and a real growth strategy, that is a document I can actually use. That stands out enormously.

Know Your Three Whys

Before any interview, you have to know your three Whys cold:

  • Why medical sales? If you cannot answer this, it is a red flag that this might be the wrong industry for you.
  • Why this company? Tie it to what you learned in your conversations and research.
  • Why you? The transferable skills that prove you will succeed in the role.

You do not need experience in the industry to answer these well. Around 12 percent of medical sales reps break in with no prior sales experience at all. Nurses, techs, and people from completely different backgrounds do it every year. What they have in common is that they tell a real story about a sales instinct, a competitive drive, or a background caring for patients, and they connect it directly to the role. Practice your three Whys until you can deliver them like you are just talking, not reciting.

The Day Of: Small Things That Decide the Outcome

The details you control are the details that separate you. Handle them:

  • Trade your phone for a notepad. Turn the phone off and leave it in the car. Bring a pad loaded with questions and take notes throughout. Having your phone out during an interview or a ride along is a deal killer.
  • Dress up. Suit and tie unless they specifically tell you not to. You can never overdress.
  • Bring a brag book. Print your rankings, awards, quota attainment, and proof of your sales success. Fewer people do this now, which is exactly why it makes you stand out.
  • Show up early and bring energy. Arrive 30 minutes early. Smile, relax, and bring real energy. No hiring manager ever passed on a candidate for being too positive.

Ask questions that point forward. Who are your top reps and what makes them your top reps? What does good look like at three months, six months, and a year? Those questions show you are already thinking about how to win in the role, and the answers tell you whether the expectations are realistic before you sign up.

Close Like a Rep, Not a Candidate

You close on every interview, but most people skip the setup. I am a Yankees fan, so think of it this way. Mariano Rivera was the greatest closer of all time, but he still had a setup man in the seventh and eighth before he came in for the ninth. Your setup question is your setup man.

At the end, when they ask if you have any questions, go with something open ended: "Bob, after talking for the past hour and learning more about what you need, what do you think?" Leave it right there. Now they sell you back to themselves by listing your strengths, and you find out what they may have missed. If they raise an objection, good. Objections are opportunities. Handle it fully before you move on, because you cannot close over an unanswered objection.

Then close on the next step. "Is there any hesitation about moving me to the next round?" And here is a bonus move. Ask for the names of the top reps so you can learn from them before the next interview. If the manager hands those names over, you are moving forward. That is one of the clearest buying signals there is.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I explain a gap in my resume?

Be truthful and do not overexplain. A gap is only a killer when your story does not add up. If you were doing other work during that time, even outside the industry, put it on the resume so it shows you kept working. Anything under six months rarely raises an eyebrow. Anything over a year needs a clear, honest explanation.

Can I break into medical sales with no sales experience?

Yes. Around 12 percent of medical sales reps break in without prior sales experience. They win by outworking everyone during the interview process and proving they can do the job through their preparation, not their past titles.

How many jobs should I apply to?

Fewer than you think. Focus on three to five openings a week where you meet at least 75 percent of the requirements, and go all in on each one. A focused, aggressive application beats 100 sprayed resumes every time.

How should I answer "why do you want to leave your job?"

Stay positive and talk about the future, not the past. Even if your last role was a disaster, do not trash it. Frame the move around the opportunity in front of you. If you speak negatively about a former employer, the interviewer assumes you will speak the same way about them later.

How do I follow up after an interview without being annoying?

Get the next steps and the decision timeline before you leave the room. Send a thank you that reiterates what you discussed, then follow up on the timeline they gave you. Be persistent and confident, but never desperate. Desperation is a turnoff that costs candidates offers.
